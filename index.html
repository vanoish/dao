Before the name

A thought shaped like water

“The highest goodness is like water. Water benefits the ten thousand things and does not contend.”

02

The word beneath the words

What is the Dao?

Dao 道 means a way, path, course, or manner of moving. In early texts it is not a distant ruler or a fixed substance. It is the generative course through which the ten thousand things arise, change, return, and find their fitting relation.

The Dao cannot be reduced to a definition because every definition selects one shape and leaves others outside. The opening of the Daodejing therefore treats naming as both necessary and insufficient: the named Dao is already a particular expression, while the unnamed remains the open source of possibility.

“A way that can be spoken is not the constant way; a name that can be named is not the constant name.”

04

A longer historical lens

From classical reflection to medieval rite

c. 4th–3rd c. BCE

The early philosophical field

The Daodejing and Zhuangzi emerge within the plural world of the Warring States. Their concern is not a single institution, but how humans might live with the changing grain of the world.

2nd–6th c. CE

Formation and ritualization

Daoist communities, scriptures, registers, healing practices, confession, meditation and salvation rites give the Way institutional and liturgical forms.

10th–14th c. CE

The medieval repertoire

The period studied by Liu is marked by war, disease, social mobility and religious competition. Buddhist and Daoist specialists adapt shared techniques to answer urgent needs of the living and the dead.

03

From ritual to religious landscape

Universal salvation was made in the spaces between traditions

Jingyu Liu’s dissertation follows a medieval Chinese world in which Buddhism, Daoism, Confucian ethics, local cults, bureaucracy and techniques of healing were not sealed compartments. They formed a shared repertoire from which ritual specialists selected, translated and recomposed effective forms.

水

The Water-Land Retreat

The Buddhist Water-Land Retreat gathered beings from the upper, middle and lower realms. Its salvific grammar joined confession, refuge, bodhisattva precepts, food offerings and Dharma teaching. Hungry ghosts, wandering souls, local gods, animals, officials and deities could enter one ritual field. Salvation was therefore not only a promise of rebirth: it was a carefully staged conversion into a Buddhist community.

FJSF and TDMY traditions · Buddhist liturgical manuals

籙

The Yellow Register Retreat

The Daoist Yellow Register Retreat addressed the dead through a different logic. Confession could remove names from registers of sin; documents moved through celestial and subterranean offices; and the rite of liandu, or sublimation, restored the body through perfected pneuma, water and fire. The dead were not merely transported elsewhere—they were cleansed, re-formed and returned to a renewed order.

Wushang huanglu dazhai licheng yi · five-day ritual sequence

“Things that concern the living are also things that concern the dead.”

This sentence offers a key to Liu’s argument. Universal salvation rites answered social anxieties—war, famine, disease, abandoned corpses and restless spirits—while also promising spiritual transformation. Their power came from combination: the Buddhist ideal of universal deliverance, Daoist techniques of exorcism and bodily refinement, Confucian filial concern, local understandings of ghosts, and the administrative imagination of paperwork, petitions and amnesty.

The dissertation is not a description of early Daoism in the fourth–third centuries BCE. It belongs to the medieval history of Chinese religion, especially the Song and Yuan worlds. Here it is presented as a later echo: a study of how early ideas of natural order, ritual efficacy and human–cosmic correspondence were transformed into large public rites for the living and the dead.

The dissertation in four movements

  1. Water-Land: conversion, confession, precepts, food and ritual paperwork.
  2. Water-Land’s esoteric current: mantras, mudrās, visualisation and altar geometry.
  3. Synthetic ritual: Daoist, Confucian, local and Buddhist elements translated into one field.
  4. Yellow Register: judicial confession, bodily restoration and liandu—sublimation before rebirth.
